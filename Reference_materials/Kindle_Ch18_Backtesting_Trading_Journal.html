Lesson 18.1 The Difference Between Feeling and Knowing

Here is the central problem of this entire field, stated plainly. The sky is enormously rich; the market moves every single day; and human memory is a champion at recalling hits while quietly discarding misses. Put those three facts together and any astrological rule you care to name will feel like it works. You will remember the time Mercury retrograde “coincided” with a sell-off and forget the four retrogrades that didn't. You will notice the eclipse that landed near a market top and never count the eclipses that landed near nothing at all.

This is not a failure of intelligence — it is simply how cognition operates. We are pattern-finding animals living in a world that throws patterns at us faster than we can audit them. And that is precisely why feeling that a method works is no evidence whatsoever that it actually does. The feeling is generated by the same machinery whether the rule is real or worthless; it cannot tell the two apart. Something outside the feeling has to do the discriminating.

The backtest and the trading journal are the two instruments that replace feeling with knowing. A backtest asks a pre-committed rule a fair question against history. A journal turns live, real-money trading into an ongoing, honest, out-of-sample test that no hindsight can corrupt. This chapter builds both. Along the way it uses a simulation to show — with numbers, not assertions — how a completely worthless rule can manufacture an impressive-looking track record purely by searching, and how only out-of-sample testing exposes the illusion. Every “it called the 2008 crash” claim in this entire field lives or dies on the disciplines assembled here.

Key Rule · 18.1 A method that feels true and a method that is true produce the identical feeling. Only a fair test — pre-committed and out-of-sample — can tell them apart.

Q2 What two instruments does the chapter offer to replace feeling with knowing?
A The backtest (a pre-committed rule asked a fair question against historical data) and the trading journal (live trading logged as an ongoing, real-money out-of-sample test).

Lesson 18.2 The Machinery of an Honest Test

Framework Five ideas separate a real test from sophisticated self-deception. None of them is optional; drop any one and the door to phantom edges swings open.

Concept 1
Pre-registration

State the complete rule before looking at the outcome: the signal (e.g. “Saturn stations retrograde”), the prediction — direction and a dated window — the universe and period, the position size and exit, and, crucially, the hit criterion (what exactly counts as success). A hypothesis written after seeing the data is not a hypothesis; it is a description.

Concept 2 · the master tool
In-sample vs out-of-sample

Develop and tune a rule on one slice of history (in-sample); then test the frozen rule on a different, untouched slice (out-of-sample). Only out-of-sample performance is evidence. In-sample success is guaranteed to anyone who tries enough variations — as §18.4 demonstrates with numbers.

Concept 3 · the full 2×2
Score the confusion matrix

A rule produces four outcomes: hits (predicted, happened), misses (happened, not predicted), false alarms (predicted, didn't happen), and correct passes (quiet predicted, quiet happened). “It called the crash” reports only the hits. The honest question is the full 2×2 against the base rate: how often does the event happen anyway, and does the rule beat that after its false alarms are counted?

Concept 4 · the look-elsewhere effect
The data-mining trap

Every time you try another planet, another aspect, another orb, another window, you run a hidden statistical test. Run enough of them and one will look brilliant by chance alone. This multiple-comparisons problem is the single biggest source of phantom astro-edges — and §18.4 quantifies exactly how big.

Concept 5 · the real-world tax
Costs, survivorship, slippage

A backtest that ignores brokerage, the Securities Transaction Tax (STT) in India, bid–ask slippage, and survivorship (delisted stocks, discontinued series) will flatter a rule that dies the moment it meets a real broker. Paper edges and net edges are different animals.

Callout · The Cardinal Rule of Evidence If it isn't pre-registered and out-of-sample, it isn't evidence. Everything else — a curve that fits the past, a rule that “called” three crashes, an elegant story — is at best a hypothesis to be tested, and at worst a flattering accident of memory and search.
Key Rule · 18.2 Pre-register, separate in-sample from out-of-sample, score the whole confusion matrix, penalise the search, and subtract real costs. Skip any one and you are deceiving yourself with style.
Check Yourself · 18.2
Q1 What four elements must a pre-registration fix before any data is examined?
A The signal, the prediction (direction and a dated window), the universe/period/sizing/exit, and — most importantly — the hit criterion that defines success. Anything specified after seeing the outcome is a description, not a prediction.
Q2 Why does “it called the crash” fail to constitute a fair test, even when true?
A It reports only the top-left cell of the confusion matrix — the hits — while ignoring misses and false alarms, and it never checks the base rate. A rule that calls three crashes but also fires on fifty quiet periods has no edge once the full 2×2 is scored.
Q3 How does trying many planets, aspects, orbs and windows secretly inflate your results?
A Each variation is a hidden statistical test. Run hundreds and one is expected to look brilliant by chance alone — the look-elsewhere / multiple-comparisons effect. The apparent “winner” is an artefact of searching, not a property of the market.

Lesson 18.3 How to Actually Backtest an Astrological Signal

Framework A defensible protocol, executed strictly in this order:

  1. Pre-register the rule in writing — signal, direction, window, hit criterion, universe, period, sizing, exit. Date it. (For an astrological rule, the “signal” might be a planetary station, an eclipse on a natal point, a dasha change, or a KP cuspal-sub-lord condition — exactly the falsifiable kind KP offers, Chapter 14.)
  2. Split the data — an in-sample period to develop on, an out-of-sample period kept sealed, and ideally walk-forward validation (roll the window forward repeatedly) for robustness.
  3. Benchmark against the right nulls — both of them — (a) buy-and-hold, and (b) a random signal of equal frequency. The second is the one practitioners forget, and the one §18.4 shows is decisive: your rule must beat not just the market but a coin that fires as often as it does.
  4. Do the statistics honestly — enough independent trials (N) to matter, report an effect size with a confidence interval, and correct for the number of rules you tried (the more variations explored, the higher the bar — conceptually a Bonferroni-style penalty, or a “deflated” performance figure).
  5. Subtract realistic costs — commissions, STT, slippage, taxes.
  6. Then, and only then, decide whether the rule has earned provisional, low-confidence use — still sized for weak evidence, still risk-capped.

Framework And the journal is this protocol's living extension. Log every signal, every call (including the trades you didn't take), the pre-stated criterion, and the honest outcome. A faithfully kept journal is a permanent, real-money out-of-sample test that no amount of hindsight can corrupt — because each entry was committed before the result was known.

Key Rule · 18.3 Pre-register, split, benchmark against both nulls, do honest statistics with a search penalty, subtract costs — and only then grant a rule weak, risk-capped, provisional use.
Check Yourself · 18.3
Q1 The protocol names two benchmarks. Which one do practitioners routinely forget, and why does it matter?
A A random signal of equal frequency. Beating buy-and-hold is not enough — a rule must also beat a coin that fires as often as it does. As §18.4 shows, a worthless rule can clear the market bar while failing this one, which is the bar that actually exposes the illusion.
Q2 Why must the statistics “correct for the number of rules you tried”?
A Because each rule tested is a separate roll of the dice; the more you try, the more likely one looks significant by chance. A multiple-comparisons penalty (Bonferroni-style, or a deflated performance figure) raises the significance bar in proportion to the search, neutralising that inflation.
Q3 What is the relationship between the backtest protocol and the journal?
A The journal is the protocol's living extension — a continuous, real-money out-of-sample test. Every pre-committed call logged with its honest outcome adds an incorruptible data point, because hindsight cannot edit a decision that was recorded before the result was known.

Lesson 18.4 The Simulation That Should Haunt Every Backtester

The claim under test

Framework Consider a claim of exactly the kind this field produces by the dozen: “I tested an astrological rule against history and it predicted turns about 54% of the time — better than a coin.” How seriously should we take such a result? The rest of this lesson answers that question not with argument but with a computation.

The simulation — a worthless rule that looks great

Observed Computed for this book. Take an efficient market — 1,000 days of pure zero-mean random returns, in which no predictive signal exists by construction. The fair-coin baseline for guessing up or down is therefore exactly 50%. Now data-mine 500 random “astro rules,” each producing a daily up/down call, and keep the single best performer:

ResultAccuracy
Fair-coin baseline50.0%
Mean of the 500 random rules50.0% (SD 1.5%)
Best in-sample rule — what one would “discover” and publish53.9%
Same rule, out-of-sample — fresh random data50.9%

Observed The lesson is brutal and exact. The best of 500 worthless rules showed a ~3.9-point “edge” over coin-flipping — sitting roughly 2.5 standard errors above 50%, a textbook multiple-comparisons inflation — on data that contained no signal at all. Out-of-sample, on fresh random data, it collapsed straight back to chance (50.9%). The edge was an artefact of searching, never a property of the world. And here is the sting: anyone who tested 500 astrological variants on real market history and reported “my best rule hits 54%” would have produced exactly this number for exactly this reason — and mistaken pure noise for genuine discovery.

Why “an aspect was present at the crash” proves nothing

Observed A second computation makes the look-elsewhere problem concrete. With 10 bodies there are 45 planet-pairs, times 5 major aspects = 225 aspect-slots, each carrying a roughly 6–8° orb. The arithmetic consequence is unavoidable: several dozen aspects are active on essentially every day. So the observation that “a hard aspect coincided with the 1929 / 1987 / 2000 / 2008 / 2020 crash” is expected for any date one cares to pick. Its evidential value is approximately zero in the absence of a pre-registered rule scored on hits and misses against the base rate. The sky is always busy; finding an aspect near any chosen event is the default, not the discovery.

What would actually count as evidence

Framework A genuine astro-edge would have to clear every one of the following bars at once:

Bar 1
Pre-registered

The full rule and hit criterion fixed in writing before any outcome was seen.

Bar 2
Beats both nulls

Outperforms buy-and-hold and a random signal of equal frequency.

Bar 3
Survives out-of-sample

Holds up on sealed out-of-sample or walk-forward data, not just the slice it was built on.

Bar 4
Reports the full matrix

States its false-alarm and miss rates against the event's base rate, not hits alone.

Bar 5
Survives the search penalty

Remains significant after correction for the number of rules tried.

Bar 6
Positive after costs

Still profitable once brokerage, STT, slippage and taxes are netted out.

This is a high bar — and, as Chapter 19 will show, essentially nothing in the financial-astrology literature has ever cleared it. The disciplines in this chapter are simply how you would find out for yourself, honestly, rather than taking anyone's word.

Callout · Beat the Coin That Fires as Often as You Do The benchmark that exposes most astro-backtests is not the market — it is a random signal of the same frequency. If your eclipse rule fires ten times a decade, compare it to ten random dates a decade, thousands of times over. If it can't beat that null out-of-sample, it has no edge — however well it “fits” the crashes you happen to remember.
Key Rule · 18.4 The best of 500 worthless rules hit 53.9% in-sample on a no-signal market and 50.9% out-of-sample. Searching manufactures edges; only sealed out-of-sample testing reveals there was never anything there.

Lesson 18.5 The Backtest Protocol and the Journal
  1. Pre-register before you peek. Write the rule, direction, window, hit criterion, universe, period, sizing and exit before testing. Date and freeze it.
  2. Split, and keep the out-of-sample sealed. Develop in-sample; validate on untouched data; walk forward if you can. Never let out-of-sample data influence the rule.
  3. Benchmark against buy-and-hold AND a random-frequency null. Beating the market is not enough; beat a coin that fires as often as your signal (§18.4).
  4. Score the full 2×2 against the base rate. Report hit rate, false-alarm rate, and miss rate — and the event's base rate. Never report hits alone.
  5. Penalise for the search. Count how many rules, orbs and windows you tried, and raise the bar accordingly (multiple-comparisons correction). One “winner” out of hundreds is expected by chance.
  6. Subtract real costs. Brokerage, STT, slippage, taxes. Marginal edges die here.
  7. Keep an honest journal as a living out-of-sample test. Log every signal, every call (and every pass), the pre-stated criterion, and the outcome — including the trades you skipped. Review on a schedule; retire rules that fail. The journal, not the backtest, is the long-run arbiter.
The Honest Frame · The Journal Is the Real Backtest A historical backtest can be gamed by hindsight; a forward journal cannot. Every honestly logged, pre-committed call is one more out-of-sample data point on whether your astrology does anything at all. Years of such entries are the most credible evidence a practitioner can ever assemble — for or against.
Key Rule · 18.5 Seven rules, in order — pre-register, seal the out-of-sample, benchmark both nulls, score the full matrix, penalise the search, subtract costs, journal forever. The forward journal outranks the backtest.
Check Yourself · 18.5
Q1 Rule 4 forbids reporting hits alone. What three numbers — plus one reference value — must accompany them?
A The hit rate, the false-alarm rate and the miss rate, all reported against the event's base rate. Without the base rate and the two error rates, a hit count is uninterpretable.
Q2 Why does the chapter call the journal — not the backtest — the long-run arbiter?
A A historical backtest can be gamed by hindsight; a forward journal cannot, because each entry is committed before its outcome is known. Years of pre-committed, honestly scored calls become the most credible body of evidence a practitioner can hold, for or against the method.
Q3 What single habit keeps a journal honest, and what happens without it?
A Logging every pass and every failed call, not just the trades taken — and reviewing on a schedule to retire rules that fail. Without it the journal silently becomes another hit-collector, recreating the exact selective-memory bias it was meant to defeat.

Lesson 18.6 Edge Cases and Cautions
  1. In-sample success is not evidence — the cardinal sin. Observed The simulation says it all: a no-signal rule hit 53.9% in-sample and 50.9% out-of-sample. A backtest without an out-of-sample test proves precisely nothing.
  2. “It called the crash” ignores the denominator. Survivorship of hits in memory, with misses and false alarms uncounted, is the field's defining error. Demand the full confusion matrix every time.
  3. The forking-paths problem. Every undocumented choice — which orb, which planet, which window, sidereal or tropical — is a hidden test. Decision freedom inflates apparent significance even without explicitly “trying 500 rules.”
  4. Overfitting and rectification. A chart rectified to past crashes, or orbs tuned until the curve fits, will always “work” in-sample and fail forward (Chapters 1, 13). Curve-fitting masquerades as discovery.
  5. Costs and slippage are edge-killers. Frequent-signal strategies look best on paper and worst after STT, brokerage and slippage. Always net them out before believing a result.
  6. Confirmation bias contaminates journals too. A journal is only as honest as its keeper. Log the skipped trades and the failed calls, or the journal becomes just another hit-collector.
  7. Rare events can't be validated. Five crashes in a century cannot confirm any rule — N is far too small. Treat every “crash-predictor” claim with this hard limit in mind.
  8. The standing caveat. Observed Backtesting and journaling are the cure, but the honest expectation — confirmed in Chapter 19 — is that most astrological market rules fail an honest out-of-sample, cost-adjusted, multiple-comparisons-corrected test. Testing protects you precisely by being willing to deliver that verdict.
Key Rule · 18.6 In-sample wins, remembered hits, undocumented choices, rectification, costs, dishonest journals, and tiny N all manufacture false confidence. The cure is testing — even when the verdict is “no edge.”
Check Yourself · 18.6
Q1 What is the “forking-paths” problem, and how does it inflate significance without anyone running 500 rules?
A Every undocumented analytic choice — orb, planet, window, sidereal vs tropical — is effectively a hidden test. The freedom to have chosen differently inflates apparent significance, because the result is implicitly the best of many unrecorded paths even if only one was ever written down.
Q2 Why can rare events such as market crashes never validate a predictive rule?
A Because N is hopelessly small — five crashes in a century cannot support any statistical inference. Any rule will appear to “fit” such a tiny sample, so crash-predictor claims should be treated as untestable on their own terms.
Q3 The chapter calls testing the “cure,” yet warns of the likely verdict. What is that verdict, and why is delivering it a feature?
A The honest expectation — confirmed in Chapter 19 — is that most astro-market rules fail an out-of-sample, cost-adjusted, multiple-comparisons-corrected test. That a method is willing to return “no edge” is exactly what makes it protective; a test that could only ever say “yes” would be worthless.

Summary · 18.7 Five Takeaways
  1. Feeling that a method works is not evidence; memory hoards hits and discards misses. The backtest and the journal are what replace feeling with knowing.
  2. Pre-registration and out-of-sample are non-negotiable: state the full rule and hit criterion before looking, develop in-sample, and judge only on sealed out-of-sample (or walk-forward) data.
  3. A live simulation proves the danger: the best of 500 worthless rules hit 53.9% in-sample on a no-signal market and 50.9% out-of-sample — an “edge” manufactured entirely by searching. Beat a random signal of equal frequency, not just the market.
  4. Score misses and false alarms against the base rate, correct for the number of rules tried, and subtract real costs (STT, brokerage, slippage) — and remember that dozens of aspects are active every day, so “an aspect coincided with the crash” proves nothing.
  5. The honest journal is the ultimate out-of-sample test — log every pre-committed call and pass, and retire what fails. Testing is the cure; the expected verdict (Chapter 19) is that most astro-rules don't survive it.

Reference Chapter Glossary
Backtest
The application of a pre-committed rule to historical data to measure how it would have performed. Only meaningful when paired with a sealed out-of-sample test. Framework
Pre-registration
Fixing the complete rule — signal, direction, dated window, universe, period, sizing, exit and hit criterion — in writing before any outcome is examined. The defence against describing the past and calling it a prediction. Framework
In-sample / out-of-sample
In-sample is the data a rule is developed and tuned on; out-of-sample is a different, untouched slice on which the frozen rule is tested. Only out-of-sample performance counts as evidence. Framework
Walk-forward validation
Repeatedly rolling the development window forward and testing on the next unseen segment, providing a more robust out-of-sample estimate than a single split. Framework
Confusion matrix (2×2)
The full table of outcomes — hits, misses, false alarms and correct passes — scored against the base rate. The honest alternative to reporting hits alone. Framework
Base rate
How often the predicted event happens anyway, irrespective of the signal. A rule earns credit only by beating its base rate after false alarms are counted. Framework
Multiple-comparisons / look-elsewhere effect
The inflation of apparent significance that arises from testing many rules: try enough and one looks brilliant by chance. Corrected by raising the bar in proportion to the search (e.g. a Bonferroni-style penalty). Observed
Random-frequency null
A benchmark consisting of a random signal that fires as often as the rule under test. The decisive null most practitioners forget — a real edge must beat it out-of-sample. Framework
Survivorship bias
The distortion from testing only on instruments that still exist, ignoring delisted stocks and discontinued series, which flatters historical results. Framework
Slippage
The gap between the assumed and realised execution price; together with brokerage and the Securities Transaction Tax (STT), it erodes or erases marginal paper edges. Framework
Forking-paths problem
Inflation of significance caused by undocumented analytic freedom — choice of orb, planet, window, or zodiac — even when only one rule is finally written down. Observed
Trading journal
A continuously kept log of every signal, call and pass with its pre-stated criterion and honest outcome — a permanent, real-money, hindsight-proof out-of-sample test. Framework
