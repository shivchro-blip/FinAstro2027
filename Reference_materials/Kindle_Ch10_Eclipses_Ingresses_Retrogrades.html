Lesson 10.1 Chapter Introduction — From the Slow Tide to the Datable Event

The great cycles tell you the tide — where the market sits in a multi-year rhythm. But a tide gives no precise hour. For sharper timing the tradition turns to events that happen on a specific date: an eclipse, a planet changing signs, a planet stopping to turn retrograde. These are the timing triggers — the layer between the slow primary-trend cycles (years) and the daily lunar clock (Chapter 6).

All three share one virtue and one vice. The virtue: they are exactly datable, so they impose a disciplined calendar of windows to watch. The vice: they are frequent — several eclipses, dozens of stations, and constant ingresses every year — so it is trivially easy to find some event near any past market turn and declare a hit. This chapter teaches the three triggers, grounds them in the actual upcoming dates, and then confronts that frequency problem head-on. The verdict, foreshadowed: treat them as watch-windows for heightened volatility, confirmed by price — never as stand-alone signals, and never with the cherry-picking the “always-on sky” invites.

Key Rule · Lesson 10.1 A trigger is a date on the calendar, not a verdict on the tape. The slow cycles say where the tide sits; the triggers say which days deserve a closer look — and nothing more until price agrees.

Lesson 10.2 Core Concepts — The Three Triggers

Eclipses — supercharged luminations at the nodes

Astronomical An eclipse is a New Moon (solar) or Full Moon (lunar) that occurs near a lunar node — which is why Chapter 9's nodal axis matters here. Because the Sun must be near a node, eclipses come in eclipse seasons roughly every ~6 months (when the Sun crosses the nodal axis), usually two, occasionally three, eclipses per season, and recur in Saros families every ~18 years. Classical Framework Both traditions read eclipses as amplified, “fated,” wild-card moments: in Vedic terms Rahu/Ketu “swallow” the luminary (a grahana, classically inauspicious — muhurta avoids them); in market lore they are watched as potential turning points, with the eclipse's sign/degree and what it aspects said to flavour the effect, which is held to unfold over the following weeks-to-months.

Ingresses — a planet changes costume

Astronomical Framework An ingress is a planet crossing from one sign into the next — a change of “costume” for the significator (Chapter 0). Two kinds matter:

  • The Sun's cardinal ingresses (0° Aries, Cancer, Libra, Capricorn — the equinoxes and solstices). In mundane astrology these are cast as charts for the season or year (the “Aries ingress” = the year-chart). The Sun's seasonal cycle is also the one with the most empirical market support (calendar seasonality, §10.4).
  • Slow-planet ingresses (Jupiter, Saturn, the outers changing signs) — these shift the significator backdrop for months or years (e.g., Jupiter leaving its exaltation, Saturn entering a new sign).

Retrograde stations — the pivot (from Chapter 7)

Astronomical Framework A station is the moment a planet stops to change direction (Chapter 7's “tradeable state”). The station date is the pivot — the cycle hinges there. Mercury's stations open the famous “Mercury-retrograde” windows (trade/communication/data-disruption lore); the slow planets' stations (Saturn, Jupiter, the outers) are the more-watched turning-point windows.

Callout — Datable, But Frequent The three triggers give dates — their great advantage over the diffuse slow cycles. But count them: ~4–7 eclipses a year, ~20+ planetary stations a year, and ingresses almost monthly. With that many flags on the calendar, some trigger sits near every market turn by chance alone. The discipline of this chapter is to respect the dates while refusing the cherry-pick.

Lesson 10.3 Classical & Historical Framework

Classical Eclipses (grahanas) are treated in Vedic astrology as powerful and inauspicious — the nodes devouring Sun or Moon — with effects judged from the eclipse's sign, the houses it activates in a chart, and the planets it aspects; auspicious activities are traditionally suspended during them. Western mundane astrology similarly treats eclipses as major markers, often assigning an “effect period” scaled to the eclipse's duration.

Classical Ingress charts are a classical mundane technique: the chart cast for the Sun's entry into a cardinal sign (especially 0° Aries — the Aries ingress) is read as a horoscope for the coming quarter or year. The four cardinal ingresses (the equinoxes and solstices) anchor the seasonal mundane calendar.

Astronomical The relevant cycles to keep distinct (the Chapter 9 “cycle zoo” warning applies): the Saros (~18 years 11 days — eclipse families), the eclipse year (~346.6 days — shorter than the calendar year because the nodes regress), and the synodic month (the new/full-Moon beat).

Framework · A Caution on Gann Framework W. D. Gann is often said to have emphasised the solstices and equinoxes (cardinal ingresses) in his market timing; the astronomical dates are real and exact, but the specific trading rules attributed to him are unreliable and apocrypha-prone — use only the verifiable dates, not the legend.
Key Rule · Lesson 10.3 The doctrine is old and the astronomy is exact — eclipses as grahanas, the Aries-ingress year-chart, the distinct Saros and eclipse-year cycles. The trading legend wrapped around them is not: keep the dates, discard the apocrypha.

Lesson 10.4 Market Correlation Analysis — The Honest Test, and the Live Calendar

The claim

Framework The proposition: eclipses, ingresses, and stations mark heightened-volatility / turning-point windows — moments when the market is more likely to reverse, accelerate, or shake out. The eclipse is the dramatic “wild card”; the slow-planet station is the cycle's hinge; the cardinal ingress opens a new seasonal chapter.

The honest evidence

Observed The triggers must be scored carefully, because their drama invites the worst cherry-picking in the field:

  • Eclipses: the popular “eclipse = crash/turn” claim has no rigorous support. With 4–7 eclipses a year and an elastic “effect period” of weeks-to-months, some eclipse sits near every notable market move — the textbook “always-on sky” illusion (Chapter 1). No demonstrated directional edge.
  • Mercury retrograde: as Chapter 7 stated, no evidence of a directional market effect. A soft disruption/choppiness flag at most — never a trade.
  • Ingresses / seasonality: the one trigger with some empirical footing is the Sun's seasonal cycle — the documented “Sell in May / Halloween” seasonality (Bouman & Jacobsen, 2002, and later work), a statistical calendar effect, modest and debated. That is a solar-calendar regularity, not eclipse-or-station magic, and even it is contested.
  • Stations: slow-planet stations are watched as hinges, but the evidence is the same small-N, post-hoc story as the great cycles — suggestive, unproven.

The genuine, defensible value is organizational: the triggers give a pre-committed calendar of dates to pay closer attention to. Combined with price structure, that focus can be useful. Standing alone, the frequency of events makes their specificity near zero.

Lesson 10.5 The Practitioner's Method — Using Event Timing
  1. Maintain a pre-committed trigger calendar. Compute and diarize, in advance, the upcoming eclipses, slow-planet stations, and cardinal (Sun) ingresses (Appendix A). Pre-commitment is what prevents after-the-fact cherry-picking.
  2. Rank the triggers by weight. Highest: slow-planet stations and cardinal Sun ingresses (and eclipses for volatility, not direction). Lowest: Mercury retrograde — a disruption flag only. Weight your attention accordingly.
  3. Assign each a pre-specified window (orb). Eclipses: a window of days to a few weeks; stations: the retrograde “shadow” span around the station; ingresses: the days around the crossing. Fix the window before the event, never stretch it afterward to capture a move.
  4. Require confluence; the trigger says “watch,” price says “act.” Act only where a trigger window coincides with a major structural level and a sentiment extreme (Chapter 3). A trigger alone is never a trade.
  5. Size for low specificity, fix risk first. Because many triggers fire each year, any single one is weak evidence. Size positions small, treat the trigger as context/attention, and set risk independently.
Key Rule · Lesson 10.5 Pre-commit, rank, bound, confirm, size small. Diarize the dates in advance, weight stations and cardinal ingresses above Mercury-retrograde, fix each orb before the event, demand price-and-sentiment confluence, and set risk independently of the trigger.

Lesson 10.6 Edge Cases and Cautions
  1. The “always-on sky” / frequency trap (the central caution). Observed With ~4–7 eclipses, ~20+ stations, and ~monthly ingresses a year, some trigger sits near every market turn by chance. This is the single biggest source of false belief in event-timing. Pre-commit your calendar and windows; never reverse-engineer a “trigger” for a move after it happens.
  2. Mercury-retrograde lore is unsupported. Observed No directional market effect is established (though Wang et al. 2021 document a belief-driven return effect during retrograde windows; see Lesson 19.4). Disruption/choppiness flag at most.
  3. Elastic effect-periods are unfalsifiable. “The eclipse's influence lasts up to six months” is so wide it guarantees a hit. Insist on a pre-stated, bounded window or discard the claim.
  4. Keep the cycles distinct. Saros (~18 yr 11 d), eclipse year (~346.6 d), nodal regression (18.6 yr), synodic month — different cycles; conflating them (Chapter 9's zoo) breeds spurious fits.
  5. An eclipse is a special new/full Moon — not every lunation. Don't treat ordinary new/full Moons as eclipses; the node-proximity is what (allegedly) supercharges them.
  6. Gann's seasonal rules are apocrypha-prone. The equinox/solstice dates are exact astronomy; the trading rules attributed to Gann are unreliable. Use the dates, not the legend.
  7. Compute exact times in your timezone. Station, ingress, and eclipse moments are precise and timezone-dependent (IST for the Indian market). Eyeballing introduces error (Appendix A).
  8. The standing evidence caveat. Observed None of these triggers has validated predictive power over markets. Their honest role is a pre-committed calendar of attention-windows, confirmed by price and governed by risk.
Key Rule · Lesson 10.6 Every caution points one way: frequency plus elastic windows manufactures false patterns. Pre-commit dates and bounded orbs, keep the cycles distinct, localize the times, and remember none of these triggers has validated predictive power.

Section 10.7 Chapter Summary — Five Takeaways
  1. Three datable triggers punctuate the slow cycles: eclipses (new/full Moons at the nodes — the bridge from Chapter 9), ingresses (sign changes — costume changes for the significator), and retrograde stations (the cycle's hinge).
  2. Their virtue is precise dates; their vice is frequency. Many fire each year, so the “always-on sky” makes cherry-picking almost irresistible — the central danger of this chapter.
  3. The honest evidence is thin: eclipse-and-station turning-point claims are unproven and post-hoc; Mercury-retrograde has no directional support; only the Sun's seasonal cycle (“Sell in May,” Bouman & Jacobsen 2002) has modest, contested empirical footing.
  4. The live calendar is busy: a Mercury-retrograde window from ~30 Jun 2026, Saturn's station ~27 Jul, a total solar eclipse 12 Aug 2026, and Jupiter leaving its exaltation ~1 Nov — diarized as watch-windows, not forecasts.
  5. A calendar of windows, not a list of trades. Pre-commit the dates and orbs, rank stations/ingresses above Mercury-retrograde, and act only on price confluence with risk fixed — the trigger says watch, the chart says act.
