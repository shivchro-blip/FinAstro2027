Does It Actually Work?

The Honest Evidence — eighteen chapters of craft, and the question answered at last: financial astrology has no demonstrated power to predict prices.
✦ ✦ ✦

The book's moment of truth. Eighteen chapters have taught the craft while repeating a single refrain — no validated edge; test it. This chapter gathers what the published research and the honest practitioner's record actually show, and answers the question directly. The verdict, stated plainly up front: financial astrology has no demonstrated power to predict prices. The few replicable effects in its vicinity — a small lunar-return pattern, a seasonal calendar effect — are behavioral and seasonal, not astrological, are contested, and are often not even tradeable after costs. The honest task is to separate what is real from what merely feels real, and to hold a fascination with this field without deceiving yourself.

Lesson 19.1 Answering the Question Directly

A book that spent nineteen chapters dodging the obvious question would not deserve the reader's trust. So: does financial astrology work? The honest answer has three parts, and this chapter defends each in turn.

✕
As a price-prediction system
No.

There is no body of replicated, out-of-sample, cost-adjusted evidence that any astrological rule forecasts markets better than chance. Chapter 18's simulation showed how easily the appearance of such an edge is manufactured by searching.

≈
In astrology's neighbourhood
A few small effects are real — but they are not astrology.

A lunar-phase return pattern and a seasonal ("Sell in May") effect have genuine peer-reviewed support. Their explanations are behavioral (mood) and seasonal, not planetary — and they are small, contested, and frequently vanish after trading costs.

✓
As a symbolic & behavioral discipline
Defensibly useful.

The value argued for throughout — a sentiment-cycle lens and a discipline scaffold (Chapter 17), confirmed by price and governed by risk — survives the evidence precisely because it makes no causal forecasting claim.

This chapter assembles the actual studies, sorts the field into what is supported, what has failed, and what is untestable, and closes on the only intellectually honest posture: stay curious, weigh the evidence ruthlessly, and never let fascination outrun proof.

Lesson 19.2 How to Weigh the Evidence

Framework Before the findings, the standards for judging them. Most arguments about whether astrology "works" go wrong not on the data but on the standard applied to it.

The evidence hierarchy

From weakest to strongest, claims earn weight in this order:

  1. Anecdote — "it called the crash." A story, not evidence. No denominator.
  2. In-sample correlation — a pattern that fits past data. Cheap to find, easy to overfit (Chapter 18).
  3. Replicated, out-of-sample effect — it holds on new data, in new markets, by independent authors. Now it counts.
  4. Effect with a validated mechanism — we know why it happens. The strongest tier of all.

Only the top two tiers carry real weight — and astrology's causal claims reach neither.

The efficient-market null and anomaly decay

Observed The default hypothesis is that liquid markets are broadly efficient, so a genuine, simple, public edge should be arbitraged away. And empirically, edges largely are: McLean and Pontiff (2016) showed that documented return anomalies shrink substantially after publication. Any claimed astro-edge must answer the awkward question — if it is real and has been public for decades, why has it not been competed into oblivion?

Three distinct questions

Framework Confusing these three is the field's commonest error:

Question 1
Is there a real statistical effect?

Sometimes yes — the lunar and seasonal patterns are real.

Question 2
Does astrology explain it?

No — the explanations are behavioral and seasonal, not planetary.

Question 3
Is it tradeable after costs?

Usually no — small, fragile effects rarely survive slippage and fees.

An honest "does it work?" must answer all three — not wave at the first and quietly imply the other two.

Callout · A Real Effect Is Not a Vindicated Theory Even if the Moon's phase correlates with returns, that does not validate astrology — it points to mood. The existence of a small effect in astrology's territory is routinely mis-sold as "science proves astrology." It does no such thing: it relocates the cause to human psychology and the calendar.
Key Rule · 19.2 Grant weight only at the top of the hierarchy — replicated, out-of-sample, mechanism-backed — and always answer all three questions: real? caused by astrology? tradeable?
Check Yourself
Q1 Where do "it called the crash" anecdotes and in-sample correlations sit on the hierarchy, and why so low?
A At the bottom two rungs — anecdotes have no denominator, and in-sample fits are cheap to manufacture by searching, so neither earns real weight.
Q2 What does the efficient-market null demand of any decades-old, public astro-edge?
A An explanation for why it has not been arbitraged away — McLean and Pontiff (2016) show that documented anomalies shrink substantially after publication.
Q3 Which of the three questions does a dishonest "does it work?" answer, and which does it skip?
A It answers only "is there a real effect?" and skips whether astrology actually causes it and whether it survives costs.

Lesson 19.3 Sorting the Field into Three Buckets

Framework With the standard fixed, the field of financial-astrology claims sorts cleanly into three buckets. The honest map looks like this:

Bucket 1
Weakly supported real effect, non-astrological cause

The lunar-phase return effect (new-moon returns > full-moon returns) and the seasonal / Halloween ("Sell in May") effect. Both have peer-reviewed support; both are explained by mood and seasonality, not planets; both are small and contested.

Bucket 2
Unsupported failed under testing

Mercury-retrograde directional lore (no evidence, Chapter 7) (though Wang et al. 2021 document a belief-driven return effect during retrograde windows; see Lesson 19.4); "a hard aspect coincided with the crash" (combinatorially meaningless — dozens of aspects active daily, Chapter 18); planetary causation of prices (no mechanism, no validated signal); and most geocosmic / aspect-timing rules when properly out-of-sample tested.

Bucket 3
Effectively untestable near-unfalsifiable

The dasha in aggregate (always in some period, Chapter 12); rectified market charts tuned to past events (Chapter 13); and small-N great-cycle claims (≈4–5 cycles per century, Chapters 8–9), whose own proponents concede frequent misses.

The buckets are not equal failures. Bucket 1 contains something real that simply does not belong to astrology; Bucket 2 contains claims that fail when tested; Bucket 3 contains claims that cannot be tested at all. Honest discussion keeps these straight rather than borrowing Bucket 1's credibility to defend Buckets 2 and 3.

Key Rule · 19.3 Three buckets, kept separate: real-but-not-astrological, failed-under-testing, and untestable. The grain of truth in the first never licenses the other two.

Lesson 19.4 The Evidence Review

This is the chapter's core: what the studies actually say — not paraphrased lore, but the published findings with their authors, their magnitudes, and their failed replications.

The lunar-phase effect — real, small, behavioral, partly-replicated

Observed The most-cited work is Dichev and Janes (2003) (Journal of Private Equity; first circulated 2001): across roughly two dozen markets over about 30 years, returns in the ~15 days around the new moon were close to double those in the ~15 days around the full moon. Independently, Yuan, Zheng and Zhu (2006) (Journal of Empirical Finance) examined 48 countries and found returns significantly lower around the full moon than the new moon — on the order of a few percent annualized — an effect not explained by trading volume, volatility, or interest rates, and stronger in markets with less institutional dominance, pointing to individual-investor sentiment. Keef and Khaled (2011) broadly confirmed the pattern internationally.

Observed But the honest caveats are decisive:

Mechanism
Mood, not astrology

The authors lean on the mood-and-returns link (e.g. Hirshleifer and Shumway on sunshine and mood): the lunar cycle plausibly nudges human mood, which nudges risk appetite. No planetary "influence" is invoked or needed.

Replication
Only half holds up

Measured against lunar control days, later work (Brusa and colleagues) found new-moon returns exceeded the control, but full-moon returns did not differ from control (p ≈ 0.88). The "depressed full moon" half of the story failed. The effect is fragile, not bankable.

Magnitude
Small and likely decaying

A modest, public, decades-old anomaly — exactly the kind McLean and Pontiff (2016) show tends to erode — and far too small to trade after costs.

Observed One more instability belongs on the record. Floros and Tan (2013) — the same study Chapter 1 cited for the effect's entanglement with ordinary calendar anomalies — examined 59 international markets over 2004–2010 and found that the generally bullish new-moon tilt inverted under acute stress: during the 2008 crisis, the full-moon window showed the higher relative returns. A systemic panic overrides a faint mood tilt without noticing it — one more reason the effect is context, never a rule.

The seasonal / "Sell in May" effect — robust-ish, contested, not astrological

Observed Bouman and Jacobsen (2002) (American Economic Review) documented that November–April returns exceeded May–October returns in 36 of 37 markets, robust over time, with UK evidence traced back centuries and no convincing conventional explanation. Zhang and Jacobsen (2021) extended this to ~109 markets across ~323 years and found the pattern persisting — it "defies economic gravity" by failing to disappear despite being public for generations.

Observed Yet it is contested precisely on the grounds this book cares about:

  • Outlier-driven. Maberly and Pierce (2004) argued the U.S. result is driven by two outliers — the October 1987 crash and the 1998 LTCM collapse — and is not robust to alternative model specifications.
  • Not exploitable after costs. Other work finds no tradeable effect once costs are included (e.g. in S&P 500 futures over 1982–2003).
  • Seasonal, not planetary. It is a calendar effect, plausibly tied to vacations, liquidity, and the tax year — its loose overlap with the solar year does not make it astrological.

The Mercury-retrograde effect — a belief, priced

Observed The newest entry in this literature is the most instructive. Wang et al. (2021) examined daily index returns across 48 countries from 1973 to 2019 and found returns roughly 3.33% lower on an annualized basis during Mercury-retrograde periods. Crucially, the study tested two rival explanations. The real-effect channel — that the apparent backward motion itself disrupts communication and commerce — was checked against aviation and U.S. road-accident frequencies (1975–2017), corporate earnings surprises, and firm-level news sentiment: no rise in real-world disruption appeared, and the channel was rejected. The investor-belief channel survived: believing the window is error-prone, a slice of investors withdraws from trading, shrinking the market's aggregate risk-bearing capacity and forcing a higher premium that depresses prices.

Observed Three further findings pin the cause to belief rather than sky. The deficit is larger in countries whose markets fared poorly during the prior year's retrogrades (interaction coefficient −3.366, t = −2.68) — outcomes feeding beliefs feeding outcomes. Online search interest in retrograde motion negatively predicts returns, and the deficit concentrates where that interest runs high — as it does in countries with deep Hellenic cultural roots and lower scientific development, where the tradition persists. And there is a volatility paradox: despite the lore of chaos, return volatility falls during retrogrades — participants sit out rather than churn, exactly the wait-and-see behaviour the belief channel predicts.

Framework Read carefully, this relocates rather than rescues the claim. The lore's mechanism — the planet disrupting affairs — was tested and failed. What is real is a return effect caused by believers: a Bucket 1 effect, mood's cousin, belonging to psychology and not to Mercury.

Callout · The Self-Fulfilling Sky Framework The belief channel generalises. If a critical mass of traders watches the same celestial parameter — a retrograde window, a lunar phase, a published planetary price level — their synchronized caution or selling produces the very move they expected, validating the belief that caused it. A scientifically empty input can thereby manufacture a real, measurable market outcome. This is the one mechanism by which astrology can genuinely move prices — and it is a fact about crowds, not planets: the effect lives exactly as long as the believers do, and owes nothing to the sky.

Weather, mood, and the behavioral thread

Observed A related literature links environmental mood cues to returns — sunshine, and debated geomagnetic and seasonal-affective effects. These are behavioral-finance findings about human psychology — the genuine grain of truth the book has pointed to since Chapter 1: markets are mass mood, and mood has rhythms. None of it confers any planetary forecasting power.

Observed The geomagnetic thread deserves its named study. Krivelyova and Robotti (2003) related geomagnetic-storm activity — solar-wind disturbances of Earth's magnetosphere, which a physiological literature links to cardiovascular stress, depressed mood, and heightened risk aversion — to U.S. and world index returns over 1970–2002, finding that unusually high storm activity exerts a significant negative effect on the following week's returns, with the strongest returns arriving in geomagnetically quiet periods. The proposed mechanism is the misattribution of mood: investors feel worse for an environmental reason, misread the feeling as economic pessimism, and rotate toward safe assets. It is the same grain of truth again — environment, mood, prices — with not a planet anywhere in the chain.

What fails

Observed On the other side of the ledger:

Mercury-retrograde lore
No directional effect

No credible evidence of a directional market effect (Chapter 7) (though Wang et al. 2021 document a belief-driven return deficit during retrograde windows; see the retrograde section above).

"An aspect at the crash"
Near-zero evidential value

With ~225 aspect-slots and dozens active any day (Chapter 18), this is expected for every date.

Planetary price causation
No validated edge

Most aspect and geocosmic timing rules show no out-of-sample, cost-adjusted, multiple-comparisons-corrected edge.

Small-N cycle claims
Unfalsifiable

McWhirter, great conjunctions: unfalsifiable at 4–5 occurrences, and their own advocates concede frequent misses (Chapters 8–9).

The synthesis

Framework Putting it together: the honest "does it work?" verdict is that astrology has no validated predictive edge, and the small real effects in its neighbourhood (1) are behavioral / seasonal, not astrological, (2) are contested and partly-unreplicated, (3) are usually not tradeable after costs, and (4) are decaying as public anomalies do. They do not belong to astrology and do not rescue its causal claims. What does survive scrutiny is the book's actual thesis: astrology as a symbolic sentiment-cycle lens and behavioral discipline — useful for process, not prophecy.

Callout · The Grain of Truth, Correctly Located Strip the field to what the evidence supports and you are left with one defensible sentence: human mood moves markets, and mood has cyclical rhythms. Astrology is, at best, an ancient and elaborate symbolic notation for those rhythms — not a mechanism, not a forecast. Credit the psychology; keep the lens; drop the causation.
Key Rule · 19.4 The strongest evidence in astrology's territory points away from astrology: real effects trace to mood and the calendar, are small and fragile, and decay once known.

Lesson 19.5 Holding a Fascination Without Self-Deception

The evidence is in. What remains is conduct: how a thoughtful person keeps a genuine interest in this subject without letting it corrupt judgment or capital. Six rules carry the whole posture.

  1. Weigh by the hierarchy. Discount anecdote and in-sample fit entirely; grant weight only to replicated, out-of-sample effects with a plausible mechanism.
  2. Demand all three answers. For any claimed effect ask: is it real? is the cause actually astrological? is it tradeable after costs? Refuse claims that answer only the first.
  3. Attribute real effects to their real causes. When a lunar or seasonal pattern appears, credit mood and the calendar, not planets. Resist "science proves astrology."
  4. Treat astrology as lens and discipline, never oracle. Use it as the sentiment-cycle framing and behavioral scaffold of Chapter 17 — confirmed by price, governed by risk — and size every position as if the astrological edge were zero (because, on the evidence, it is).
  5. Make your journal your evidence. Your own pre-registered, scored record (Chapter 18) is the most relevant data you will ever have; let it — not tradition or hope — update your beliefs, and be willing to conclude "this doesn't work."
  6. Stay curious, stay unattached. Fascination with the symbolism is legitimate; attachment to the conclusion is not. Hold the subject the way an honest scientist holds a beautiful hypothesis the data won't support: with affection and without illusion.
Callout · The Honest Practitioner's Creed I find this lens beautiful and useful for discipline. I have no evidence it predicts prices, and I trade as if it does not. I test what I can, I count my misses, and I follow the evidence even when it disappoints me. That posture is the whole book in five sentences.
Key Rule · 19.5 Keep the fascination; refuse the self-deception. Credit real causes, size as if the edge is zero, let the journal rule, and hold the conclusion loosely.

Lesson 19.6 Edge Cases & Cautions

Seven traps catch people who have read the evidence and still talk themselves past it. Each is a place where an honest reading quietly slides into a hopeful one.

  1. Mistaking a neighbourhood effect for a vindication. Observed "A journal found a lunar effect!" does not mean astrology works; it means mood has a rhythm. Keep the logical step honest.
  2. Anomaly decay. Observed Even real, published effects (lunar, seasonal) tend to shrink once known (McLean & Pontiff, 2016). Yesterday's documented edge is not today's tradeable one.
  3. Survivorship of famous "hits." The crashes astrology "called" are remembered; the far more numerous non-events are not. Always restore the denominator (Chapter 18).
  4. The seduction of the small-but-real. A genuine ~few-percent, fragile, behavioral effect is easily inflated into a trading religion. Real ≠ large ≠ tradeable.
  5. Contested findings are contested. The seasonal effect is outlier-sensitive (Maberly & Pierce, 2004) and often not exploitable after costs; the full-moon half of the lunar effect fails against control days. Cite the whole literature, not the flattering half.
  6. Conviction outrunning evidence. Framework The gap between "interesting lens" and "I am sure" is where capital — and judgment — are lost. This is the bridge to Chapter 20's ethics and risk.
  7. The standing verdict. Observed No validated predictive edge; the real nearby effects are behavioral / seasonal, small, contested, decaying, and non-astrological. The defensible use is symbolic and behavioral — confirmed by price, governed by risk.
Key Rule · 19.6 Every trap is the same move in disguise: borrowing a small real effect's credibility, or a remembered hit's drama, to license a conviction the evidence won't bear.
Check Yourself
Q1 "A lunar effect was found, so astrology works." Name the broken logical step.
A The effect points to mood having a rhythm, not to planetary causation — the conclusion smuggles in a mechanism the finding never established.
Q2 Why is "real" not the same as "tradeable"?
A A genuine effect can still be too small and fragile to survive costs, and can decay once it is public — real, small, and untradeable all at once.

Summary · 19.7 Five Takeaways
  1. Does financial astrology predict prices? No. There is no replicated, out-of-sample, cost-adjusted evidence of an edge, and the appearance of one is easily manufactured by searching (Chapter 18).
  2. Two real effects sit in astrology's neighbourhood: the lunar-phase return pattern (Dichev–Janes 2003; Yuan–Zheng–Zhu 2006) and the seasonal / Halloween effect (Bouman–Jacobsen 2002; Zhang–Jacobsen 2021) — but they are behavioral and seasonal, not astrological.
  3. Both are small, contested, and fragile: the depressed-full-moon half fails against control days; the seasonal effect is outlier-driven (Maberly–Pierce 2004) and often not tradeable after costs; and public anomalies decay (McLean–Pontiff 2016).
  4. Astrology's own causal claims fail or are untestable: Mercury-retrograde lore, "an aspect at the crash," planetary price-causation, and small-N cycle claims do not survive honest scrutiny.
  5. The grain of truth, correctly located: mood moves markets and mood has rhythms. Astrology is at best a symbolic notation for those rhythms and a discipline scaffold — useful for process, not prophecy. Stay curious; never let fascination outrun the evidence.

Checklist · 19.8 Weighing an Astro-Market Claim

Before you accept — or repeat — any claim that astrology moves markets, run it through these seven checks.

  • 1. Where on the hierarchy? Anecdote / in-sample / replicated-out-of-sample / mechanism — and am I granting weight only to the top tiers?
  • 2. All three questions answered? Is the effect real, is the cause actually astrological, and is it tradeable after costs?
  • 3. Cause correctly attributed? Have I credited mood and seasonality rather than leaping to "astrology works"?
  • 4. Whole literature, not half? Have I included the failed replications and the critiques — control-day, outlier, after-cost?
  • 5. Decay considered? If the effect is real and public, why hasn't it been arbitraged away?
  • 6. Sized as if edge = zero? Am I trading the position on its risk merits, not on an unproven astrological edge?
  • 7. Journal updating my beliefs? Is my own pre-registered, scored record — including misses — what actually moves my confidence, and am I willing to conclude "it doesn't work"?

Glossary — Chapter 19

Evidence hierarchy Framework
The ranking of claims from weakest to strongest: anecdote < in-sample correlation < replicated out-of-sample effect < effect with a validated mechanism. Only the top two tiers earn real weight.
Out-of-sample Framework
Tested on data not used to find the pattern — a different period, market, or author. The minimum bar for a correlation to count as evidence rather than a fit.
Efficient-market null Observed
The default assumption that liquid markets price in public information, so simple, public edges get arbitraged away. The benchmark any claimed astro-edge must beat.
Anomaly decay Observed
The empirical tendency of documented return anomalies to shrink substantially after publication (McLean & Pontiff, 2016). A real, public, decades-old edge should already have eroded.
Lunar-phase effect Observed
The finding that returns near the new moon exceed returns near the full moon (Dichev–Janes 2003; Yuan–Zheng–Zhu 2006). Real but small, attributed to mood, and only partly replicated against control days.
Seasonal / "Sell in May" effect Observed
The pattern of higher November–April than May–October returns across most markets (Bouman–Jacobsen 2002; Zhang–Jacobsen 2021). A calendar effect, contested as outlier-driven (Maberly–Pierce 2004) and often untradeable after costs.
Control days Observed
A replication method comparing effect-window returns against a matched baseline. The test that showed the lunar effect's "depressed full moon" half does not differ from control (p ≈ 0.88).
Survivorship of "hits" Framework
The bias by which remembered correct calls are counted while the more numerous non-events are forgotten. Corrected by always restoring the denominator (Chapter 18).
Grain of truth Framework
The one defensible claim left after stripping the field: human mood moves markets and mood has cyclical rhythms. Astrology is at most symbolic notation for those rhythms, not their cause.
Process, not prophecy Framework
The book's defensible use of astrology — a sentiment-cycle lens and behavioral discipline that organises attention and risk, making no forecasting claim about prices.
