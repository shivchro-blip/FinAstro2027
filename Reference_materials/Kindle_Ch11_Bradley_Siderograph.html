The Bradley Siderograph & Aspect-Sum Models

The most famous quantitative tool in financial astrology — and the most honest. Where every earlier chapter read the sky one aspect at a time, Donald Bradley summed the entire sky into a single daily number, producing a continuous curve whose turns flag candidate reversal dates. This is where quantification meets candor.

✦ ✦ ✦

In 1948 Donald Bradley did something audacious. Where every previous chapter has read aspects and cycles one at a time — this square, that station, this eclipse — Bradley summed the entire sky's aspects into a single number for each day, producing a continuous curve: the siderograph. It is the field's closest thing to a model. It is also attached to the field's most honest author, who insisted the curve shows the timing of turns, not the direction or size of market moves — and disclaimed it as a literal predictor. This chapter is where the book's two preoccupations meet: rigorous quantification, and the candor to refuse to oversell it.

Lesson 11.1
Chapter Introduction — Summing the Whole Sky

Chapters 8 through 10 read the sky qualitatively: this cycle, that eclipse, this retrograde station. Bradley's insight was to make it quantitative. In Stock Market Prediction (1948) he proposed converting every aspect between every pair of planets into a number — weighted by the aspect's type and tightness — and adding them all up for each day. The result is a single time series, the siderograph (or "sidereal potential"): a wiggly curve stretching a year ahead, whose peaks and troughs Bradley offered as candidate market turning-point dates.

It is the most influential attempt to turn astrology into a model — pre-committed, computable, dated, and therefore falsifiable. And it arrives wrapped in exactly the humility this book prizes. Bradley himself stressed that the curve indicates when turns may cluster, not which way the market will go or how far — and explicitly warned against treating it as a literal forecast. This chapter explains how the siderograph is built (with a transparent reconstruction you can see), what it legitimately claims, where its falsifiability quietly leaks, and how a disciplined practitioner would use — and, crucially, test — it.

Key Rule · 11.1The siderograph is one number per day: the weighted sum of the whole sky's aspects. Its peaks and troughs are candidate turning-date windows — not a forecast of where price goes.

Lesson 11.2
Core Concepts — The Aspect-Sum Idea

The core move

Framework The siderograph is built in three conceptual steps:

①
Score every aspect
Measure & weight

For each pair of planets, measure their angular separation and assign a number based on the aspect — conjunction, square, opposition, trine, sextile — and how tight it is (the orb). Hard and soft aspects contribute differently.

②
Sum across all pairs
Add to one total

Add every pair's contribution into a single daily total — the day's "potential." Nine bodies give thirty-six pairs; each day's number folds them all together.

③
Plot it over time
Trace the curve

The daily totals trace a continuous curve across the year. Its local highs and lows are the turning-point candidates you read.

Historical Bradley's actual construction was more elaborate than this skeleton. He combined a long-term term, a middle-term term, and a short-term term, plus a declination component, with his own aspect weights and orbs. Those specific weights are his; the idea — a weighted aspect-sum curve — is what we reconstruct transparently below.

The zodiac-independence payoff (Chapter 2, cashed in)

Framework Here is a beautiful consequence. Because the siderograph is built entirely from angles between planets, it is identical in the tropical and sidereal zodiacs — exactly the aspect-invariance proven in Chapter 2. The siderograph never asks what sign a planet is in; it asks only how planets are angled to each other. So this most-Western of tools transfers to an Indian sidereal practice with no conversion whatsoever — a concrete demonstration of Chapter 2's central insight.

What it claims — and what it does not

Historical Framework The siderograph's claim is narrow and specific: the turn dates of the curve — its local highs and lows — are candidate market reversal windows. It explicitly does not claim that the curve's direction matches the market's (a rising siderograph does not mean a rising market), nor that the curve's level or amplitude maps to price.

Only the timing of the turns is offered. This narrowness is both its discipline and — as §11.6 shows — its falsifiability weakness.

Callout — Turn Dates, Not a Trend Line The single most common misuse is to read the siderograph as a forecast of the market's path — buying when the curve rises. That is not what it claims. It is a calendar of candidate reversal dates; the curve's own up/down is not the market's up/down. Read the turns, ignore the slope.
Key Rule · 11.2The siderograph is pure angle-math, so it is identical tropical or sidereal — and it offers turn dates only: not direction, not magnitude.

Lesson 11.3
Historical Framework — Bradley and the Honest Tradition

Historical Donald A. Bradley — who also wrote under the name "Garth Allen" — introduced the siderograph in Stock Market Prediction: The Planetary Barometer and How to Use It (1948). His model summed weighted aspects, with declination and long-, middle-, and short-term components, into the annual curve, published ahead of time as a barometer of when market turns might cluster.

Historical What makes Bradley the moral center of this book's stance is his candor. He repeatedly cautioned that the siderograph shows the timing — the "tenor" — of potential turning points, not a literal price path; does not specify direction or magnitude; and should not be taken as a guaranteed forecast.

And the tool's real-world record is, by all honest accounts, mixed — some years the turn dates lined up strikingly, others they did not, and Bradley himself walked back any claim to literal prediction. Framework That posture — build a precise, falsifiable, pre-committed model, then refuse to oversell it — is exactly the discipline this entire book is trying to teach. Bradley is worth studying as much for his humility as for his math.

Historical Successors and variants followed: commercial siderograph publishers, and a broader family of "aspectarian-sum" / financial-astrology barometer models — including modern "composite" or "aspex" curves. They differ in their weights and components, which means — importantly — they do not all produce the same curve (§11.6).

Key Rule · 11.3Bradley's lasting lesson is not the math but the manner: a precise, pre-committed, falsifiable model, openly disclaimed as no guaranteed forecast.

Lesson 11.4
Market Correlation — The Honest Test, and a Reconstructed Curve

The claim, restated

Framework Turn dates of the siderograph ≈ windows when a market reversal is more likely. The practitioner watches those dates for a turn, confirmed by price. Nothing more is offered, and nothing more should be read in.

The honest evidence

Observed The siderograph's scorecard is genuinely double-edged.

✓
In its favour
Best-formed hypothesis

It is transparent, computable, dated, and pre-committed — everything an astrological claim should be to deserve testing. It is the field's best-formed hypothesis.

✕
Against it
No demonstrated edge

There is no robust, independently-validated edge. Worse, two structural problems blunt it: many turns per year, and a direction-free claim that makes naive "hits" too easy.

The two structural problems deserve naming. First, the curve has many turns per year — the frequency, "always-on" problem of Chapter 10 — so some turn sits near most market wiggles by chance alone. Second, because direction is not claimed, almost any move near a turn date can be glossed as a "hit," which makes naive scoring meaningless. Add Bradley's own disclaimer and the mixed record, and the honest verdict is: a falsifiable, interesting model with no demonstrated predictive power — to be backtested, never trusted on faith.

Astronomical Note — The Two Anchors, Verified The two bracketed turns rest on independently checkable astronomy. Verify A live sidereal (Lahiri) ephemeris confirms Saturn stations retrograde on 27 July 2026 at 20.5° Pisces — its motion reverses from direct to retrograde to the day — placing it squarely under the ~28 Jul curve low. And Swiss Ephemeris confirms a total solar eclipse on 12 August 2026 (sidereal Sun 25.6° Cancer, lunar node 5.6° Aquarius), whose eclipse season the ~27 Aug high brackets. The dates are real; the question §11.4 presses is what their coincidence with the curve is worth.

Framework Two honest observations follow.

1. Convergence with Chapter 10 — and why it is not independent confirmation. The ~28 Jul turn sits right on Saturn's retrograde station (Chapter 10), and the ~27 Aug turn brackets the 12 August total solar eclipse and its eclipse season. This looks like multiple tools "agreeing" — but they agree because they are built from the same underlying astronomy. A siderograph turn near an eclipse is not an independent second vote; it is the same sky counted twice. Treating overlapping astrological tools as independent confirmations is a subtle but serious error.

2. The discipline. These are candidate reversal-date windows — direction unknown, to be watched and confirmed by price — not predictions that the market turns on 28 July or 27 August. And because this is a reconstruction, a real practitioner should compute a properly-specified siderograph and, above all, backtest it on their own market (Chapter 18) before granting it any weight.

Callout — The Falsifiability Leak A model that predicts a turn but not which direction is hard to score honestly: a small dip, a small rally, or a sideways wobble near the date can all be claimed as a "turn." Unless you pre-define what counts as a hit — a reversal of a stated size within a stated window — and score every date, misses included, the siderograph's apparent accuracy is an illusion. Rigorous scoring (Chapter 18) is not optional here; it is the whole game.
Key Rule · 11.4The siderograph is the field's best hypothesis and its least-proven one. Overlapping tools near a date are the same sky counted twice — never independent confirmation.

Lesson 11.5
The Practitioner's Method — Using a Siderograph
  1. Compute (or obtain) a properly-specified curve, and name it. Use a real siderograph — Bradley's components, or a clearly-defined aspect-sum — and record which construction it is, because they disagree (§11.6). Never eyeball.
  2. Extract turn dates, ignore the slope. Read the curve's local highs and lows as candidate reversal dates. Disregard the curve's direction and amplitude — they are not the market's.
  3. Assign each turn a pre-specified window (orb). Fix, in advance, how many trading days around a curve-turn count as the window. Pre-commitment prevents after-the-fact stretching.
  4. Require a real price setup at the date. Act only where a turn-date window meets a genuine structural level and a sentiment extreme (Chapter 3). The siderograph says when to look; price says whether to act.
  5. Don't double-count the sky. When a siderograph turn coincides with an eclipse or station, recognise it as the same astronomy, not independent confirmation. Weight it once.
  6. Backtest before trusting, then size for weak evidence. Score the curve's turn dates honestly against your market's history — hits and misses, pre-defined (Chapter 18). Until it earns its keep out-of-sample, treat it as context only, with risk fixed independently.
Key Rule · 11.5The siderograph says when to look; price says whether to act. Name the curve, pre-fix the window, count the sky once, and backtest before you trust.

Lesson 11.6
Edge Cases and Cautions
  1. Direction is not claimed — so score it rigorously or not at all. Observed The siderograph's silence on direction makes loose "it called the turn" claims almost unfalsifiable. Define a hit precisely — a reversal of stated size within a stated window — and score every date, or the tool's accuracy is illusory.
  2. Many turns per year → cherry-picking. Like the triggers of Chapter 10, a year's siderograph has numerous turns; some will sit near most market moves by chance. Pre-commit dates and windows.
  3. There is no canonical curve. Historical Caution Bradley's weights, a vendor's, and this chapter's reconstruction all differ and disagree. A "siderograph turn" is meaningless unless you state which construction produced it. (The curve here is explicitly illustrative.)
  4. Curve-direction ≠ market-direction (the classic misuse). Buying because the siderograph rises is a misreading. Read turns, not slope.
  5. Overfitting the weights. Tuning aspect weights until the curve fits past turns is curve-fitting (Chapter 1), guaranteeing in-sample "success" and out-of-sample failure.
  6. Overlapping tools are not independent votes. Siderograph + eclipse + station near the same date is one astronomical configuration counted several ways — not three confirmations. Do not inflate confidence from shared astronomy.
  7. Honor Bradley's own humility. Historical The tool's creator disclaimed literal prediction. Any modern user claiming more certainty than Bradley did has departed from the evidence — and from his example.
  8. The standing caveat. Observed The siderograph has no validated predictive power. Its honest worth is as a transparent, falsifiable, testable calendar of candidate turn dates — confirmed by price, proven only by backtest, governed by risk.
The Honest Caution The deepest trap is the one Bradley himself warned against: claiming more than the curve says. Because direction is unclaimed and turns are frequent, the siderograph is unusually easy to flatter and unusually hard to falsify. The remedy is not more belief but more rigor — pre-defined hits, every miss counted, no curve trusted until it survives out-of-sample testing on your market.
Key Rule · 11.6Every weakness traces to one root: a frequent, direction-free curve is easy to flatter and hard to falsify. Pre-commitment and backtesting are the only honest answers.

11.7 · Summary
Chapter Summary — Five Takeaways
  1. The siderograph sums the whole sky's weighted aspects into one daily curve (Bradley, 1948) — financial astrology's most quantitative, pre-committed, falsifiable tool.
  2. It is zodiac-independent — pure aspect math, identical tropical or sidereal — so this Western tool transfers to Indian practice unchanged (Chapter 2 realised).
  3. It claims turn dates only — not direction, not magnitude. Reading its slope as the market's path is the classic misuse; read the turns, ignore the trend line.
  4. Its falsifiability leaks precisely because direction is unclaimed and turns are frequent — so it must be scored rigorously (pre-defined hits, all misses counted) and backtested, or its accuracy is an illusion.
  5. Bradley is the model of honesty: he built a precise model and refused to oversell it. The tool has no proven edge; overlapping tools near a date are the same sky, not independent confirmation — use it as testable context, confirmed by price.
11.8 · Checklist
Practitioner's Checklist — Working a Siderograph
  • Which construction? Are you using a properly-specified curve (Bradley's, a vendor's, or a defined aspect-sum) and have you recorded which?
  • Turns extracted, slope ignored? Reading local highs/lows as candidate reversal dates, not the curve's direction as the market's?
  • Windows pre-specified? A bounded orb (in trading days) fixed for each turn before the fact?
  • Hit defined, misses counted? A precise reversal criterion set, with every turn date scored — successes and failures?
